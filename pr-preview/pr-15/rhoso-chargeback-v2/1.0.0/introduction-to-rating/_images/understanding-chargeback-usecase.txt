Let’s explore the chargeback use case within the Red Hat OpenStack Services on OpenShift, or RHOSO, environment.

To start, let’s define what "chargeback" actually means. At its core, chargeback is the process of allocating the costs of IT services and resources back to the specific business units, departments, or projects that consumed them. While this traditionally involved tracking physical assets like hardware and licenses, in a dynamic cloud environment like RHOSO, chargeback focuses on quantifying the usage of virtual resources, such as CPU, RAM, storage, network bandwidth, and specific cloud services.

There are four primary goals for implementing chargeback in a cloud environment:
First, cost recovery, which allows you to recoup operational expenses from internal consumers.
Second, cost visibility and transparency, which helps you identify exactly who is consuming which resources, fostering better management decisions.
Third, resource optimization, as users are more likely to optimize their consumption when they are aware of the associated costs.
And finally, budgeting and planning, by providing the data necessary for accurate future capacity planning.

To deliver these capabilities, RHOSO Observability integrates CloudKitty, which is the OpenStack Rating service. CloudKitty is an OpenStack-native project designed specifically to collect, process, and rate telemetry data. You can enable CloudKitty through the telemetry operator in your RHOSO environment. This integration is powerful because it bridges the gap between telemetry and FinOps, transforming raw data into structured, reliable information. It also provides granular data for financial decisions and allows administrators to define highly customizable rating rules that align with specific business models.

The chargeback process in RHOSO follows four clear steps. 
First is data collection, where RHOSO Observability continuously gathers usage metrics, such as CPU hours, RAM, storage, and network egress, from your environment.
Second is the application of rating rules. Administrators define these rules to specify how different consumptions are priced, which can account for variables like resource type, project, or even the time of day.
Third is charge calculation. CloudKitty uses these rules to calculate the monetary charges for each project or tenant.
Fourth is data availability. These charges are exposed via API endpoints, which can then feed into your external FinOps or billing systems for analysis and reporting.

Now, there is an important consideration regarding the observability platform that you must keep in mind. The platform does not guarantee 100% metrics delivery. Metrics are exposed for scraping and are not cached; therefore, if data is dropped, there is no mechanism to retrospectively backfill those gaps. 

This is a critical point because any missing data can lead to inaccuracies in your financial and chargeback reporting. Because the platform does not provide historical data recovery, it is essential that you understand the potential impact of these data gaps on your reporting accuracy. We recommend that you develop internal reconciliation strategies or establish clear expectations with your stakeholders regarding the potential for minor variations in your data.

By understanding these fundamentals, you can effectively leverage the chargeback features in RHOSO to gain better control and transparency over your cloud infrastructure costs.
