Welcome to this overview of the chargeback process flow within RHOSO. At its core, the chargeback process is a three-step workflow designed to transform raw infrastructure usage into actionable cost data. These steps are: setting rating rules, collecting metrics, and finally, generating rating reports.

Let’s look at the first step: defining and setting rating rules. This is perhaps the most critical part of the process, as these rules act as a translation layer. They convert abstract technical usage data, such as CPU hours or storage consumption, into concrete monetary values. Without these rules, raw metrics lack the necessary financial context.

When you configure rating rules within CloudKitty, you specify how different resource types and their attributes should be costed. For example, you might define a rule that charges five cents per CPU hour for a specific virtual machine flavor, or one cent per gigabyte per month for block storage. These rules can be highly granular, allowing for differentiation based on resource type, specific attributes like flavor ID, or even time-based usage. Setting these rules is essential for providing a clear pricing model, allowing for flexible cost allocation, and enabling accurate cost recovery. A useful tip to keep in mind is that this process is foundational; complex scenarios often involve multiple, layered rules to accurately reflect varying service costs and agreements.

Moving on to the second step, we have the collection of usage metrics. Once your rating rules are established, the system requires a continuous stream of usage data to process. RHOSO’s Rating service integrates directly with the underlying OpenStack monitoring infrastructure to gather these metrics. Specifically, CloudKitty consumes performance and usage data generated by services like Ceilometer, which tracks metrics such as CPU utilization, disk I/O, network traffic, and instance uptime. This ingestion process ensures that chargeback calculations are based on actual, observed resource consumption, providing an objective basis for your billing.

The third step is generating chargeback reports. In this phase, the CloudKitty rating engine continuously evaluates incoming usage metrics against your active rating rules. It then calculates the cost associated with each resource's consumption. The output is structured data that includes details like tenant ID, resource type, the consumption period, and the final calculated rate.

To retrieve these insights, you can query the CloudKitty API. For instance, the `report/summary` endpoint allows you to pull aggregated chargeback summaries. If you use a command like the one shown in our documentation, which utilizes a `curl` request, you will receive a JSON object. This output cleanly presents the resource type, time period, and total calculated costs. In an example scenario, a specific project’s CPU usage over a one-month period might show a total charge of 125.50 units, representing the cost incurred.

Beyond the API, you can also use the OpenStack command-line interface to generate these reports in various formats, such as CSV, JSON, or YAML, depending on your workflow requirements. These reports are the primary tool for financial reconciliation, providing the transparency needed for internal billing and cost analysis.

I must include a critical warning here: while CloudKitty generates detailed reports, it acts strictly as a visibility and rating engine. It does not actively enforce budgets or block the creation of new resources if a tenant exceeds a cost threshold. Its role is to provide the data, not to manage administrative policies.

Finally, we close the loop by integrating these reports with your enterprise financial systems. By funneling this structured data directly into your existing FinOps or billing solutions, you successfully connect your infrastructure consumption to your organizational cost accountability.
